LatentMesh · research prototype · v0.2.0

We tried to let AI agents skip talking.

It didn't work. The more useful discovery was that one of our own negative results was wrong — and our measuring stick was the reason.

Six pre-registered experiments. All six say the same thing: handing a model's raw internal state to another model changes what it finds likely, and never changes what it answers.

Right now, agents talk in sentences

When one AI agent hands work to another, it has to write out its reasoning first. The second agent reads those words and rebuilds its own understanding from scratch.

If both are the same kind of model, that looks wasteful. A rich internal state gets flattened into a sentence, and something poorer gets rebuilt on the other side.

So why not skip the words?

That was the idea behind LatentMesh. Capture the numbers the first model is actually thinking with, hand them straight to the second, and skip the write-it-out-and-read-it-back step entirely.

It's an appealing premise. Denser, faster, no translation loss. We spent months testing whether it works.

Something happens. It just isn't the thing that matters.

When we inject one model's state into another, we can measure a clear effect: the receiver shifts what it considers likely. That's real and it's easy to detect.

But its actual answer doesn't change. The needle moves on one dial and sits still on the other.

This matters more than it sounds. A dashboard tracking the left gauge would show a strong, statistically significant, entirely genuine effect — and it would mean nothing for what the system does.

We tested it six ways. Six nulls.

One injection point. Then two. Then we trained the receiving model to accept the payload, at three different sizes.

Every test was powered — it had enough data to detect an effect if one existed. One run came close enough to be interesting. None crossed.

Plain text, on the very same receiver, worked decisively.

the part where we were wrong

Our own control was hiding the signal

Along the way we reached a second conclusion: that the receiver wasn't sensitive to what a message said — only to the fact that something had been injected at all.

That came from comparing a real message against random noise. And random noise is wrong in two different ways at once.

Noise is wrong twice over

Random noise is meaningless, and it's also shaped unlike anything the model produces internally.

So when a real message beat it, we couldn't say which of those two things it had beaten. And when it didn't beat it, we concluded there was no signal at all.

The comparison we were missing

Take a genuine message from a different problem. Same shape, same size, same statistics — but the wrong answer for the question at hand.

Now only one thing differs. If the receiver responds differently, it's responding to meaning.

The signal had been there all along

Against a different real message, the effect is unmistakable. Against random noise, on the very same run, it disappears.

The noise was so unlike a real message that it caused its own large, generic disturbance — big enough to drown out the thing we were trying to see.

We didn't quietly rewrite the old conclusion. We appended a note narrowing it and left its numbers untouched.

why this matters beyond us

Most "does this help?" tests are too weak

Almost everyone compares X against nothing. That's the trap we fell into, and it's everywhere.

If your comparison differs from the real thing in more than one way, a win tells you nothing about which difference caused it — and a loss can hide a real effect entirely.

The stronger test is X against a realistic-but-wrong X:

Retrieved documents

Compare against documents retrieved for a different question — not against no documents.

A system prompt

Compare against a plausible different system prompt, not against an empty one.

A memory layer

Compare against someone else's memories, same shape and volume.

If it can't beat the realistic-but-wrong version, it isn't doing what you think it's doing.

A geometry surprise, found for four minutes of CPU

We wanted a fourth test case: right meaning, wrong shape. It turns out that in a 1536-dimensional space, pushing a message off its natural shape almost inevitably destroys its meaning at the same time. The two properties are nearly inseparable.

We found that with a check on a laptop CPU before spending any GPU time on an experiment that would have produced an uninterpretable result.

what we ended up with

The pipe was never the problem

Our negative result killed one payload type. It never touched the infrastructure built to test it — and that turned out to be the larger half of the project.

Receipts that can embarrass you

Every experiment stores the comparisons that could contradict it — not just the flattering ones. That's what caught the mistake above.

the honest scorecard

Judged by its own premise, this failed

Six powered experiments say the latent channel doesn't carry decisions. That's the headline and we're not softening it.

Judged by what it produced, it's a measuring instrument that caught its owners being wrong — including about one of their own negative results.

What is not claimed

ClosedUntouched
Direct injection of one model's state into another, under this setup, at the sites and sizes tested. Latent communication in general. Trained-fusion methods like Cache-to-Cache. Transfer between different models. None were tested here.

One control in the final design — the "other-example message" — is prior art from arXiv:2607.26773 and is cited as theirs, not claimed as ours. Two follow-up experiments are documented and deliberately not run; each needs its own pre-registration first.

A tool that only ever confirms your hopes isn't a measuring instrument. This one told us our headline idea didn't work — and then told us one of our own negative results was our fault rather than reality's.
